Water damage in Old Town

Old Town is Mukilteo's oldest neighborhood. It runs along the waterfront near the ferry landing and Lighthouse Park and climbs the hill toward Mukilteo Boulevard. Local real estate guides describe a mix of older houses, townhomes and condos, with a median build year around 1961. That mix means no two jobs look alike.

A leak in an older house often starts in a supply line, a drain, a roof or a window that has had years of wear. A leak in a condo or townhome can travel through a shared wall or ceiling to a neighbor. In both cases the first hours matter most, because water keeps moving into drywall, insulation and subfloor.

What makes the waterfront different

Parts of the Old Town shoreline sit close to Possession Sound. The City of Mukilteo's surface water plan says the FEMA-mapped floodplain in the city is limited to a small section of the Puget Sound coastline, around Lighthouse Park and the urban waterfront. Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status not verified for any single street.

To see whether an address is mapped, use the FEMA Flood Map Service Center and ask the city's permit center. A mapped or unmapped address does not tell you whether a pipe, roof or drain will fail, so treat every wet building the same way.

Common causes near the ferry and the hill

  • Roof leaks during long Pacific Northwest rain, often found as a stain on an upper-floor ceiling.
  • Supply line, water heater or appliance failures inside kitchens, laundry rooms and bathrooms.
  • Drain and sewer backups, which need different handling than clean water.
  • Wind-driven rain pushing water past old window and door seals on exposed blocks.

If a storm brought water in from outside, see our flood damage restoration page. For a failed pipe, read about burst pipe water damage.

If water is spreading in Old Town, do this first

Stop the source

Shut off the fixture valve or the main water valve. If you cannot find it, leave the area and call your water utility.

Power only if it is safe

Never touch a breaker or switch while standing in water or on a wet floor. If water is near the panel or outlets, stay out and call the utility or 911.

Protect people and pets

Keep everyone out of rooms with sagging ceilings or standing water. Sewage or flood water is not safe to touch.

Document, then call

Take photos of the damage and the source before you move anything. Then call (425) 592-3399 and describe what you see.

What restoration involves

A crew finds the source, removes standing water and checks wet materials with moisture meters. Wet drywall, insulation and flooring are dried or removed depending on how wet they are and how long they have been wet. Industry guidance is published as ANSI/IICRC S500, and you can ask any provider how its work follows it.

For clean pipe water, our water mitigation page explains the drying steps. For active water, see emergency water removal. Dry materials are then repaired. See water damage repair for that stage.

Contaminated water and mold

Sewage, toilet overflow and floodwater are not safe to dry with household fans or a shop vacuum. Fans can spread contaminants, and the materials may need removal. Call a professional for sewage backup cleanup. If damp materials stay wet for days, mold can grow, and no company can promise that it will not.

Insurance and paperwork

Take dated photos and keep receipts for emergency steps. Ask your insurer what your policy covers and what it requires. Coverage and claim decisions are made by the insurer, not by a restoration company. The Washington Insurance Commissioner publishes consumer guidance on property claims.
