How a storm gets water into a Mukilteo house

Puget Sound storms rarely look like a flood. They look like rain driven sideways for hours, a limb across the gutter line, or a downspout that dumped beside the foundation. Water finds its way past window trim, door thresholds, siding seams and vent penetrations that stay dry in an ordinary shower.

This page covers that kind of loss, not rising water and not a failed roof. A roof leak is covered on our roof leak water damage page, and rising water belongs with flood damage restoration. Most storm calls fall in between, with wind, wet walls and overwhelmed drains.

Safety steps during and right after the storm

  1. Stay away from downed lines and anything touching them, then call 911 and Snohomish County PUD.
  2. If water reaches outlets or the panel, do not enter. Cut power only if you can reach the panel while standing on dry floor.
  3. Smell gas? Leave the house and call 911, then Puget Sound Energy, before calling us.
  4. Run a portable generator outdoors, at least 20 feet from the building, per the Washington Insurance Commissioner.
  5. Do not wade through water that may carry sewage or live current.
  6. Keep children and pets out of wet rooms and away from broken glass.

What the crew does once the rain stops

Extraction comes first. Truck-mounted and portable extraction units remove standing water from carpet, pad and hard floors, since a wet floor will otherwise feed moisture into the walls above it. A moisture meter and thermal camera then check drywall, trim and subfloor beyond the visible stain.

Air movers push dry air across wet surfaces, and LGR dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the room. A few days is typical, though materials and weather change that. If the water came with mud, debris or runoff, the crew also washes and sanitizes surfaces and may use an air scrubber with HEPA filtration.

Ask any restoration company whether its crews follow ANSI/IICRC S500, the published standard for water damage. Wind-driven rain is usually clean at first, but runoff that crossed soil or a yard can move it into a higher category. The category sets what can be dried and what gets removed.

Gutters, drains and the stormwater system

The City of Mukilteo says its Surface Water Utility maintains city-owned stormwater infrastructure, while private ponds and underground vaults belong to the property owner. If you share a retention vault with neighbors or an association, call whoever manages it. The city's Fix It Public Works request is where residents report an existing drainage problem in a public system.

Check your own downspouts and yard drains in the first dry window after a storm. A drain that tied into a crushed pipe or a full vault can keep sending water to the same wall. Fix the water path before the rebuild, or the next storm repeats the loss.

The wettest month and why it matters here

NOAA's 1991 to 2020 climate normals for Paine Field put November as the wettest month at about 5 inches. That lands right after summer, when gutters hold leaves and sump pumps have sat idle for months. Clean gutters, test the sump pump, and look at downspout extensions in October.

Snohomish County's 2025 hazard mitigation plan describes a bomb cyclone windstorm that toppled trees into homes. That is a countywide record, not a Mukilteo statistic. For you, it means large trees near the house are worth an arborist's look before the season starts.

What changes the cost of a storm loss

  • How many rooms and how much wall height got wet
  • Whether the water was clean rain or carried yard runoff
  • Carpet and pad versus hard flooring and subfloor
  • Insulation and drywall that need to come out
  • How long the water sat before extraction began
  • Whether the power was out and equipment needs generator support

Wind damage, flood damage and your policy

Insurance splits storm losses by how the water arrived. Rain that came in through a wind-opened roof or window is often treated differently from water that rose from the ground. Flood damage is usually excluded and needs separate flood coverage, and FloodSmart notes most homeowners and renters policies do not cover it.

The Washington Insurance Commissioner's winter weather guidance says to call your insurer or agent as soon as possible and avoid permanent repairs until the insurer has seen the property. Photograph everything and keep receipts. Nobody can promise what your policy will pay, so ask your insurer and check your policy language.

Mistakes after a storm

  • Waiting for the sky to clear before calling anyone
  • Running a generator in the garage or near a window
  • Closing up wet walls with new paint or trim
  • Removing storm debris before photographing the damage
  • Assuming a dry carpet surface means a dry pad
  • Skipping the drainage fix once the room is dry
