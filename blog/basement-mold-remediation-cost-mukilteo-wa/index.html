Basement Mold Remediation Cost in Mukilteo, WA

What actually moves the price of a basement mold job, and how to read two quotes side by side.

By the Mukilteo Water Damage Pros team · Published 2026

☎ Call (425) 592-3399

What moves the price of basement mold work

Basement mold remediation cost depends mainly on how much material is affected, what that material is, and whether the water source has been fixed. No honest company can name a total over the phone, because the answer is often behind a wall or under a floor. A crew needs to see the space, and scope and price are explained before work starts.

Mold needs moisture to grow, so the leak, the seepage or the humidity has to be handled first. Remediation that follows the published ANSI/IICRC S520 standard is built around that order: find the moisture, contain the area, remove what cannot be cleaned, dry everything, then verify the result. Our mold remediation work is organized the same way.

Questions about how mold might affect anyone in your household belong to a physician. A contractor can describe the work and the price. A doctor answers the health side.

Why area and material swing the price

Surface area sets the baseline. A stain the size of a dinner plate is a different job from a wall discolored from floor to ceiling, even if both began with the same slow drip. More area means more labor and more of everything else.

Material matters just as much. The EPA notes that porous items like ceiling tiles and carpet may have to be thrown away if they are moldy. Concrete is easier to clean where it stands. Wood framing sits in between, and how it is handled depends on how deep the staining goes.

The visible patch rarely shows the full extent. Moisture meters and a thermal camera help a crew see how far wet material runs behind the surface. That is why a price built from a photo can change after a visit, and why a clear written scope matters more than a fast number.

Water source, containment and air filtration

If the water source is still active, the job can start over once the crew leaves. A failed pipe, a clogged drain or seepage along a foundation wall has to be repaired or at least identified before remediation begins. A quote that skips this step is pricing a cleanup that may not last.

Containment is the other big line item. Crews hang plastic barriers and set up negative air, so air flows into the work zone rather than out of it. They also run air scrubbers with HEPA filtration. This keeps disturbed material from drifting upstairs, and it takes equipment and setup time, so it shows up in the price.

Demolition and disposal come next. Removed drywall and insulation are bagged, carried out and hauled away. Narrow stairs or a long carry to the truck add labor, so access is a real cost factor even though it has nothing to do with the mold itself.

Why two quotes for one basement can differ

Two quotes for the same basement can land far apart without either company being dishonest. One may include containment and air scrubbers while the other assumes you will tolerate dust. One may price demolition and disposal while the other leaves the haul-out to you.

Drying is another gap. One quote may count two days of equipment and another six, and the honest answer is that it depends. Some quotes assume the source is already fixed, and some leave the rebuild out entirely. A low number that leaves out half the work is not a lower price.

How to compare two quotes

Put the quotes side by side and ask each company the same questions. The list below works for most basement jobs.

  1. Does the written scope name the rooms and materials to be removed, cleaned or dried?
  2. Is the water source fixed, or is that assumed to be someone else's job?
  3. Which containment is planned: plastic barriers, negative air, HEPA air scrubbers?
  4. Who handles demolition, bagging and disposal, and is it included?
  5. How many days of drying equipment are assumed, and what happens if readings are not dry yet?
  6. Is clearance testing included, left out, or left to an independent party?
  7. Does the quote stop at demolition, or does it include the rebuild?

Also ask whether the crew works to ANSI/IICRC S520. A written scope you can read in five minutes is a good sign. A vague one is a reason to ask more questions.

Insurance, the rebuild and a smaller scope

Insurance usually covers mold only when it follows a covered sudden water loss, such as a burst pipe. Gradual leaks and neglect are often excluded, and flood damage is usually excluded unless you carry separate flood coverage. Check your policy and ask your insurer before work begins, since insurers have a contractual right to inspect.

The Washington Insurance Commissioner's guidance suggests photographing damage when it is safe and keeping small samples of materials you discard for the adjuster. Those habits also help a restoration crew write an accurate scope.

The rebuild is its own phase. Once drying is done, replacing drywall, insulation, trim and flooring falls under water damage repair, and you can price it with the same company or a different one. Decide that before demolition so the opening is left in a state the next crew can work with.

You can also keep the scope from growing. The EPA advises drying water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours, so act on a wet basement quickly. Mukilteo local note: Permits and contractor checks

According to the City of Mukilteo's Permit Center page, building, engineering, fire and planning permits go through an online Permit Portal, and the Planning Department answers how the rules apply to a specific property. Electrical permits are handled by Washington L&I, not the city. If your rebuild after demolition touches walls, wiring or structure, ask the city building department whether a permit applies.

City Hall is open Monday to Thursday and closed Fridays, so plan your call for early in the week. Washington requires all construction contractors to register with L&I (lni.wa.gov), so you can ask any contractor for its registration and look it up before you agree to a scope.
